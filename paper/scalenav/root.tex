\begin{abstract}
Vision-based local planners react quickly to observed geometry but lack the
persistent state needed to preserve route decisions, while open-vocabulary
perception operates more slowly and may recognize hazards beyond reliable
depth.  We present ScaleNav, a layered aerial navigation framework that
couples mapping, semantic routing, and local execution across these scales.
A policy-independent mapper constructs a bounded, persistent graph of verified
free-space bubbles and collision-checked witness paths from depth and odometry.
The route layer projects open-vocabulary image responses into provisional
far-field frontiers, evaluates continuous semantic risk along graph witnesses,
and retains the accepted route in world coordinates across asynchronous graph
updates.  The resulting witness is encoded as an ordered corridor of clearance
bubbles that conditions local execution without changing the YOPO primitive
generator: online, a frontier-conditioned YOPO policy refined by an
ordered-bubble MPC; offline, a corridor-conditioned network trained on the
same contract.  Semantic observations modify route costs but do not certify free
space; geometric verification and depth-based local execution remain
authoritative.  In the latest 140-m closed-loop batch, ScaleNav completes all
ten trials at $4.134\pm0.141$~m/s with zero collisions.  On
a paired 1,479-route offline benchmark, corridor conditioning reduces
collision from $8.05\%$ to $0.14\%$ and corridor violation from $50.10\%$ to
$25.22\%$, while raising average progress from $6.225$ to $6.502$~m.
\end{abstract}

\section{INTRODUCTION}
Unknown-environment flight couples processes that should not run at the same
rate.  A local policy must react to depth and vehicle motion at control rate,
whereas map construction, long-horizon route selection, and open-vocabulary
perception evolve more slowly.  A one-stage neural planner can generate a
dynamically feasible trajectory quickly \cite{yopo}, but one observation does
not preserve an early left--right decision around a long obstacle.  RGB can
also reveal a thin or distant risk beyond valid depth, yet a reactive policy
has no spatial memory in which to retain that evidence.

ScaleNav makes this hierarchy explicit.  Its first layer is a plug-and-play
persistent mapper: generic depth and odometry are converted into a bounded
free-space graph independently of the downstream trajectory policy.  Its
second, slower layer performs semantic route planning on that graph.  Verified
bubbles form the geometric backbone, while current depth-clipped observations
form provisional semantic frontiers and continuous risk fields.  The layer
stores the selected witness in world coordinates and remaps it after graph
replacement.  Its third, fast layer is a corridor-conditioned local policy
that receives the selected route through a fixed contract---frontier goal plus
ordered bubbles---and retains responsibility for executable motion.

This separation defines the safety boundary precisely.  The mapping layer
validates measured free-space witnesses.  A provisional semantic-frontier
connection is only an optimistic route hypothesis and is checked against all
currently known obstacles before publication; it is not proof that unobserved
space is free.  Semantic risk changes route preference, while current depth
and the local executor remain responsible for newly observed obstacles.

The main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \setlength\itemsep{0pt}
  \item \textbf{A plug-and-play persistent mapping layer.}
  A bounded free-space graph turns generic depth and odometry into reusable
  route structure without an occupancy grid, ESDF, or dependence on the local
  policy architecture.
  \item \textbf{A slow semantic route layer.}
  Provisional far-field hypotheses and continuous witness risk support early
  route preference, while a world-frame witness with progress-gated
  replacement preserves route identity across asynchronous graph updates.
  \item \textbf{A fast corridor-conditioned execution layer.}
  A fixed frontier-goal and ordered-bubble contract transfers the selected
  route to a YOPO-style executor---corridor-constrained refinement online
  and a corridor-trained network offline---which retains its dynamically
  parameterized primitive set for local execution.
\end{itemize}

The evaluation separates system integration, local route adherence, and graph
persistence.  All reported semantic observations use PEARL.

\section{RELATED WORK}
Reactive planners trade global consistency for latency: RAPPIDS queries a
depth image \cite{rappids}, NanoMap retains a short range-history
\cite{nanomap}, EGO-Planner avoids ESDF construction \cite{egoplanner}, and
learned policies directly generate motion \cite{yopo,loquercio}.  Fast-Planner
and FASTER combine online search with trajectory optimization
\cite{fastplanner,faster}; SUPER advances this line with safety-assured
point-cloud corridors above $20$~m/s \cite{super}.
ScaleNav does not reproduce SUPER's guarantee or speed.  It adds the orthogonal
interface of text-conditioned route memory above a local executor.

Persistent exploration begins with frontier selection \cite{yamauchi} and
extends to incremental frontiers and hierarchical roadmaps \cite{fuel,tare}.
OctoMap stores probabilistic occupancy \cite{octomap}, Voxblox stores distance
fields \cite{voxblox}, and receding or clustered free-space corridors provide
lighter planning structure \cite{bubble,epic}.  Their costs remain geometric.
ScaleNav instead preserves a selected witness across graph replacement and
attaches open-vocabulary risk to verified witnesses while representing
beyond-range observations explicitly as provisional frontiers.

Open-vocabulary models align text with image regions \cite{clip,pearl} and can
be lifted into 3-D maps \cite{openscene}.  VLFM selects
semantic frontiers, RayFronts retains beyond-range semantic rays, and WildOS
scores sparse graph frontiers for object search \cite{vlfm,rayfronts,wildos}.
ScaleNav addresses avoidance: risk repels a route continuously along its
witness, including before depth reaches the observation.

\section{LAYERED SYSTEM OVERVIEW}
Let the vehicle state at time $t$ be
$\vect{x}_t=(\vect{p}_t,\vect{v}_t,\vect{a}_t,\vect{R}_t)$.
The sensors provide an RGB image $I_t$, a depth image $D_t$, and
odometry.
The mission gives a world-frame goal $\vect{g}$ and a text query $q$ that
describes regions to avoid.
No prior geometric map is available.

The planner must output a dynamically feasible local trajectory $\tau_t$.
Following the layered organization in Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}, mapping,
route selection, and execution are written as
\begin{align}
  \mathcal{G}_t
    &= \Pi_{\mathrm{map}}(\mathcal{G}_{t-1},D_t,\vect{x}_t), \\
  (\mathcal{W}_t,\vect{g}^{\mathrm{front}}_t,
   \vect{g}^{\mathrm{loc}}_t,\mathcal{B}_t)
    &= \Pi_{\mathrm{route}}(\mathcal{G}_t,I_{0:t},q,\vect{g}), \\
  \tau_t
    &= \Pi_{\mathrm{exec}}(D_t,\vect{o}_t,\mathcal{B}_t).
\end{align}
Here $\Pi_{\mathrm{map}}$ is the plug-and-play interface: it consumes only
depth, odometry, and its previous graph state, and exposes $\mathcal{G}_t$
without assumptions about the local policy.  The slower
$\Pi_{\mathrm{route}}$ produces the accepted world-frame witness
$\mathcal{W}_t$ and its fixed-size corridor $\mathcal{B}_t$.  The fast
$\Pi_{\mathrm{exec}}$ is demonstrated with a YOPO-based policy.  Its network
receives one $160\times96$ depth channel, body-frame motion, and the frontier
goal:
\begin{equation}
 \vect{o}_t = [\vect{v}^{B}_t,\vect{a}^{B}_t,
                (\vect{g}^{\mathrm{front}}_t-\vect{p}_t)^{B}] \in \R^9,
 \qquad
 \mathcal{B}_t\in\R^{12\times4}.
\end{equation}
The ordered corridor $\mathcal{B}_t$, whose $12$ rows contain bubble center
and safe radius, conditions execution alongside the network: in the deployed
online stack it constrains an ordered-bubble MPC refinement of the selected
primitive, while the corridor-conditioned variant evaluated offline appends
the same $12\times4$ features to the network input
(Section~\ref{sec:execution}).  The local policy does not
create a new frontier or change the global
homotopy.  It chooses a dynamically feasible primitive inside the accepted
route.

We use a fixed-height planning layer in the current experiments to isolate the
horizontal branch-selection problem.
The depth sensing, point memory, and local trajectory execution remain 3-D.
The proposed semantic cost is defined on 3-D witness polylines and does not
require the fixed-height assumption.

\section{PLUG-AND-PLAY PERSISTENT MAPPING}
\label{sec:topology}
This layer defines the reusable mapping boundary of ScaleNav.  It requires a
registered depth or point-cloud stream and odometry, and publishes an atomic
graph snapshot containing persistent node identities, verified free-space
bubbles, and edge witnesses.  Route objectives and local-policy features stay
outside the mapper, so the same graph interface can serve another route or
execution module.

\subsection{Bounded Geometric Memory}
Depth is back-projected into obstacle points and free-ray evidence.
The implementation voxel-filters the current frame and retains at most
$N_{\max}$ points; no points from earlier frames are kept in this buffer.
It does not construct occupancy voxels or an ESDF.

Following prior bubble-graph construction \cite{epic}, collision-free bubbles
are clustered into graph vertices.
A vertex is
\begin{equation}
 v_i=(\vect{c}_i,\rho_i,z_i,h_i),
\end{equation}
where $\vect{c}_i$ is its center, $\rho_i$ is free-space clearance, $z_i$ is
semantic state, and $h_i$ is a persistent identity record.
An edge stores a collision-checked witness polyline
\begin{equation}
 \pi_{ij}=\{\vect{w}^{ij}_0,\ldots,\vect{w}^{ij}_{M_{ij}}\},
 \quad
 L_{ij}=\sum_{m=1}^{M_{ij}}
 \|\vect{w}^{ij}_m-\vect{w}^{ij}_{m-1}\|_2.
\end{equation}
The witness is important twice.
It is the executable geometric connection, and it is the support on which
semantic distance is measured.
The chord $\vect{c}_j-\vect{c}_i$ is insufficient because a valid witness may
bend around an obstacle.

Geometry is updated in a background graph.
After local bubble generation, region clustering, and graph connection, an
atomic swap exposes the new graph to planning.
The point budget and spatial radius bound memory.
Transiently missing bubbles receive a short grace period before deletion.

\subsection{Clearance-Aware Edge Cost}
Let $\bar\rho_{ij}$ be the minimum cached clearance of the two endpoint
bubbles and their witness, and let $\rho^\star$ be the target clearance.
The implemented soft clearance term is
\begin{equation}
 C_{\mathrm{clr}}(e_{ij})=
 \lambda_{\mathrm{clr}}L_{ij}
 \left(\frac{\rho^\star}{\rho^\star+\bar\rho_{ij}}\right)^2.
\label{eq:clearance}
\end{equation}
It prefers open routes but does not declare a narrow valid edge invalid.
Geometric feasibility remains determined by the bubble graph and its witness
checks.

\section{SLOW SEMANTIC ROUTE LAYER}
\label{sec:semantics}
\subsection{Semantic Observation Model}
The frozen PEARL model maps $(I_t,q)$ to a patch heatmap.
The heatmap is mean-pooled on a $5\times3$ grid, and only the middle row is
retained for the fixed-height planning layer, giving one candidate per
horizontal column.  A lower-quartile frame baseline, capped at $0.25$, is
computed for diagnostics; the deployed per-column score is the raw patch
mean, which preserves the relative column ordering within the frame.
Every retained patch is projected by the calibrated pinhole model at a fixed
optical-axis depth $\ell_s$, independent of the measured depth:
\begin{equation}
 \vect{y}_{u,t}=\vect{p}_t+\mat{R}_t
 \left(\vect{t}_{\mathrm{cam}}+\ell_s\widetilde{\vect{d}}_u\right),
\label{eq:projection}
\end{equation}
where $\vect{p}_t$ and $\mat{R}_t$ are the body pose,
$\vect{t}_{\mathrm{cam}}$ is the camera translation, and
$\widetilde{\vect{d}}_u=[1,x_u,y_u]^\top$ is the unnormalized pinhole ray.
Thus every endpoint has the same optical-axis depth, while off-axis endpoints
have a larger Euclidean range. Measured depth is not consulted by this
projection and therefore cannot suppress semantic evidence.
The tuple $(s_i,c_i)$ stores the per-column score and a confidence on a
persistent node identity.  An FOV-margin term modulates confidence, and the
suspected-ground term is inactive on the fixed planning layer; timestamp
gates reject stale frames.  The deployed online
association uses the latest matched observation rather than temporal score
averaging, while graph replacement restores the stored tuple by persistent
identity.

\subsection{Provisional Semantic Frontiers}
Each retained column in a frame becomes a graph node with unknown geometry
state: every column always emits a fixed-depth virtual frontier anchor, and a
measured projection is added when synchronized depth is valid, so at most ten
candidates are admitted per frame.  Repeat observations within $r_m$ update an existing identity.
Older unknown nodes remain stored, but only the current semantic generation
participates in route search; verified nodes retain their associated semantic
state.  Unknown history is pruned behind the vehicle and capped at 512 nodes.

A current unknown node is attached to at most two of its four nearest verified
backbone candidates.  This straight connection is a provisional frontier
hypothesis, not a measured free-space edge.  It may terminate a route search
but cannot be used to connect two verified corridors through unknown space.
Before commitment, the complete candidate witness is checked against all
currently known obstacles; a failed provisional connection is detached.
When measured geometry later grows a Bubble at the node position, the unknown
node is replaced by a verified node while retaining its semantic identity.

A far-field semantic node is therefore neither a hard obstacle nor a free-space
certificate.  Low-risk rays can propose a forward frontier, whereas high-risk
rays repel nearby witnesses through the field below.

\subsection{Continuous Risk on Witness Paths}
Endpoint-only node costs react too late.
A risky semantic node may sit beyond a fork while neither endpoint of the
fork has a high semantic score.
We therefore measure every semantic node against the complete witness
polyline:
\begin{equation}
 d_{k,ij}=\min_{\vect{x}\in\pi_{ij}}
             \|\vect{y}_k-\vect{x}\|_2.
\label{eq:anchor_distance}
\end{equation}
The measured endpoint risk is
\begin{equation}
 r^{\mathrm{node}}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(s_i c_i+s_j c_j).
\end{equation}
Semantic node $k$ induces the edge risk
\begin{equation}
 r^{\mathrm{ff}}_{k,ij}=s_kc_k
 \exp\left(-\frac{d_{k,ij}^2}{2(R_s/2)^2}\right),
 \qquad d_{k,ij}\le R_s.
\label{eq:field}
\end{equation}
The implementation ties the Gaussian bandwidth to its finite support,
$\sigma_s=R_s/2$, rather than exposing two independently tuned spatial
scales.
The combined risk uses a maximum rather than a sum:
\begin{equation}
 r_{ij}=\max\left(r^{\mathrm{node}}_{ij},
                  \max_k r^{\mathrm{ff}}_{k,ij}\right).
\label{eq:risk}
\end{equation}
This avoids making risk depend on the arbitrary density of nearby semantic
nodes.
The semantic edge penalty is the barrier
\begin{equation}
 C_{\mathrm{sem}}(e_{ij})=
 \lambda_{\mathrm{sem}}L_{ij}[-\log(\max(\epsilon,1-r_{ij}))].
\label{eq:semantic_cost}
\end{equation}
It is nearly linear at low risk and grows sharply near unit risk, while the
clamp $\epsilon=10^{-3}$ keeps the planner finite for saturated observations.
The factor $L_{ij}$ makes exposure accumulate with travel distance.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Rolling Search and Route Memory}
\subsubsection{Edge and Path Objectives}
For an edge $e_{ij}$, the geometric traversal cost stored by the graph is
$W_{ij}$.  The deployed configuration does not discount remembered edges
during a fresh search; route persistence is enforced by the progress-gated
replacement described below.  The complete candidate edge cost is
\begin{equation}
 C(e_{ij})=W_{ij}+C_{\mathrm{clr}}(e_{ij})+C_{\mathrm{sem}}(e_{ij}).
\label{eq:edge_cost}
\end{equation}
For a graph path $P$, we separately accumulate its geometric and risk terms:
\begin{align}
 G(P)&=\sum_{e\in P}W_e,\\
 R(P)&=\sum_{e\in P}
       \left[C_{\mathrm{clr}}(e)+C_{\mathrm{sem}}(e)\right].
\end{align}
Keeping them separate is important: terminal selection may weight geometry
and accumulated risk differently, while neither term receives a hidden
remembered-edge discount.

The slow layer maintains three goal levels: the \emph{mission goal}
$\vect{g}$, a route-level \emph{frontier goal} selected inside the local
graph window of radius $R_g$, and a control-level \emph{local goal} on the
accepted witness.
The mission goal may lie outside the currently connected graph.
We therefore search all reachable non-viewpoint vertices within radius
$R_g$.
Terminal selection is a single soft loss: only disconnection, collision,
insufficient clearance, or a missing witness is a hard rejection.
For candidate $v$, let $d_g(v)$ be its Euclidean distance to the mission
goal.  The implemented frontier-goal score is
\begin{equation}
 J(v)=\frac{G(P_v)+R(P_v)+w_g d_g(v)}{s_g}
      +w_q\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}(s_vc_v),
\label{eq:terminal}
\end{equation}
where $s_g=\max(1,\|\vect{g}-\vect{p}_t\|_2)$ normalizes the geometric
scale, $w_g=2.0$ weights the remaining mission distance, and the final
term, with $w_q=1.0$, applies only when the terminal is itself an active
semantic frontier.  The search interface also exposes direction, FOV, and
smoothness penalties; the deployed configuration disables them with zero
weights.  Once the mission goal enters the local window, a latched
direct-goal mode validates the final connection within a short range.
Graph A* uses $f=g+h$ with millisecond-scale connection budgets.
The best recovered path ends at
\begin{equation}
 v_t^\star=\arg\min_{v\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{cand}}}J(v),
\end{equation}
and its terminal becomes the frontier goal.

\subsubsection{Route Identity Across Graph Updates}
Incremental graph replacement can change node objects even when the physical
route is unchanged.
ScaleNav therefore stores the selected witness in world coordinates:
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{W}_{t-1}=\{\vect{w}_0,\ldots,\vect{w}_K\}.
\end{equation}
The vehicle is projected onto this polyline to track progress along the
accepted route.  The complete route segment is then held intact until the
projected progress reaches a fraction $\rho_r=0.4$ of its initial length or
the witness fails its geometric check; only then does a fresh rolling search
run.  A successful search replaces the entire segment unconditionally---old
and new paths are never stitched and graph nodes are never remapped---while
a failed search holds the previous complete segment.
Equation (\ref{eq:edge_cost}) therefore ranks every fresh candidate without
a historical edge discount, and no incumbent--challenger comparison is
needed.

Semantic state survives graph replacement independently of the route: the
$(s_i,c_i)$ tuples are keyed by persistent node identity and are restored
into each new graph.  Because edge costs already contain
$C_{\mathrm{sem}}$, every triggered search evaluates the current language
evidence, while the progress-gated hold suppresses left--right oscillation
between triggers.

\paragraph{Asynchronous contract and complexity.}
The geometry, semantic, and control streams run on separate clocks.  Every
RGB, depth, odometry, graph, and semantic record carries a source timestamp;
the planner consumes a consistent snapshot rather than joining callbacks by
arrival order.  A graph worker builds topology and witnesses from the latest
bounded depth buffer, then publishes them through an atomic replacement.  A
planning tick therefore sees either the previous complete graph or the next
complete graph, never a partially rebuilt set of vertices and edges.

At each tick, semantic state is read together with the graph snapshot and the
remembered world-frame witness.  Timestamp and freshness gates reject stale
state, while a valid incumbent remains eligible during a short graph grace
period.  The progress-gated hold can consequently preserve a physical corridor
across replacement without retaining pointers into the old graph.  If the
incumbent fails its geometric witness check, the planner drops the held route
before searching again; semantic state can rank a replacement but cannot make
the failed witness executable.

Let $V$ and $E$ denote the vertices and candidate edges in the local graph,
$M$ the retained semantic nodes, and $Q$ the number of samples used to measure
each witness.  A naive semantic edge evaluation is $O(EQM)$, but the planner
first restricts nodes to the edge influence radius, so the practical work is
proportional to the local semantic neighborhood rather than the full history.
The bounded point buffer, graph window, and semantic-node cap give memory
growth $O(N_{\max}+V+E+M)$ independent of flight duration.  These bounds keep
the slow route layer from competing with the fixed-rate local controller.

\section{FAST CORRIDOR-CONDITIONED EXECUTION}
\label{sec:execution}
A frontier goal communicates where to move but not which homotopy to preserve;
an endpoint-conditioned policy can therefore shortcut away from the accepted
witness.  ScaleNav publishes a 15-m local goal and conditions execution on an
ordered corridor extracted from the same witness.

The primitive lattice follows the released YOPO parameterization.  The
$160\times96$ depth feature map is decoded on a $3\times5$ grid, giving
$N_{\mathrm{prim}}=15$ candidate directions.  Each candidate predicts a
nine-dimensional terminal state $[\vect{p}_1,\vect{v}_1,\vect{a}_1]\in\R^9$
and one scalar score.  A fixed-duration boundary-value solver converts that
state to a smooth polynomial primitive.  ScaleNav changes neither this solver
nor the candidate set.

Each corridor row contributes its body-frame center and safe radius, giving
$12\times4$ route features.  Short witnesses repeat their terminal bubble so
every row remains geometric and ordered.  An invalid or stale route provides
zero route features and recovers the frontier-only YOPO input.

The witness is refined with fixed endpoints and protected turns; an adjustment
is accepted only when every segment stays safe and bottleneck clearance
improves.  We then sample ordered bubbles
\begin{equation}
 b_k=(\vect{c}_k,r_k),\quad
 r_k=\max\left(0,d(\vect{c}_k)-r_{\mathrm{rob}}-r_{\mathrm{safe}}\right),
\label{eq:bubble}
\end{equation}
where $d(\vect{c}_k)$ is the obstacle distance at center $\vect{c}_k$.
Equation (\ref{eq:bubble}) is the offline training contract; the deployed
online pipeline keeps the mapper's clearance radius, clipped to
$[0.05,3.0]$~m, and enforces vehicle size through depth-based collision
certification and the MPC constraints below.
The execution layer receives depth, body velocity and acceleration, the
frontier goal, and a
fixed-$K$ ordered bubble list:
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{B}_t=\{([\vect{c}_k^{B}]_t,r_k)\}_{k=1}^{K},
 \qquad K=12,
\label{eq:route_input}
\end{equation}
sampled at fixed arc-length anchors along the witness.  The contract also
carries route identity, timestamp, frame, remaining length,
quality, and validity.  Invalid routes provide zero route geometry and recover
the frontier-only input.
Figure~\ref{fig:corridor_policy} summarizes the resulting local-policy
interface and primitive selection.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{pics/corridor_conditioned_yopo.pdf}
\caption{Corridor-conditioned local execution.  The accepted witness is
refined into $K=12$ ordered clearance bubbles.  In the corridor-trained

\texttt{frontier\_goal}, and the ordered corridor enter the YOPO

obstacles.}
\label{fig:corridor_policy}
\end{figure}

For the corridor-conditioned variant, the network trunk additionally consumes
the $12\times4$ route features, while the trajectory generator remains
unchanged; training adds a route term over sampled primitive points:
\begin{equation}
 L_{\mathrm{cor}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}
 \min_k\max\left(0,\|\vect{p}_n-\vect{c}_k\|_2-r_k\right)^2.
\label{eq:corridor_loss}
\end{equation}
The witness is geometric, not a timed expert trajectory: the graph selects the
corridor and the policy retains all 15 primitives within it.  Training keeps
YOPO's original terms and adds (\ref{eq:corridor_loss}) over 100 samples per
primitive, with an additional weight of $2.0$ on the maximum violation.  No
behavior cloning is used.

\paragraph{Deployed online variant.}
The current online deployment pairs the released frontier-conditioned YOPO
weights---depth and $\vect{o}_t$ only---with an ordered-bubble MPC
refinement.  The network scores the 15 primitives; only the top-scored
terminal state is passed to a constrained trajectory generator that refines
the motion inside stage bubbles sampled from the corridor, and an explicit
bubble-violation check rejects solutions that leave it.  An invalid or stale
route disables the refinement and recovers frontier-only execution.  The
corridor-trained network above is evaluated offline in
Section~\ref{sec:experiments}; all closed-loop flights use the MPC variant.

\section{EXPERIMENTS}
\label{sec:experiments}
The evaluation follows the three-layer design: closed-loop trials test the
complete stack, an offline benchmark isolates fast corridor execution, and a
round-trip analysis probes mapping and route persistence.  Closed-loop and
round-trip trials use UE Map2 through Colosseum/AirSim \cite{airsim},
synchronized RGB-D and odometry, and a 1.6-m planning layer.  One-way trials
fly from $(0,0,1.6)$ to $(0,140,1.6)$~m.  Each aggregate row contains ten
independent resets; existing batches are not seed matched, so we report sample
statistics without paired significance claims.  Failures remain in outcome
rates, and continuous flight metrics use successful trials only.

\subsubsection{Scenarios}
The current Map2 scene contains trees, long walls, and block structures along
the 140-m mission corridor.  All methods in the main comparison use this same
scene and mission endpoints.  Figure~\ref{fig:map2_speed_trajectories} uses the
point-cloud reconstruction recorded with the latest ScaleNav batch; statistics
from the superseded UE truth export are not carried into this comparison.
Matched language trials additionally include the unseen query ``avoid power
lines.''

\subsubsection{Metrics}
Closed-loop metrics are success, collision, timeout, path length $L$, flight
time $T$, $\bar v=L/T$, and maximum speed.  Completion metrics ($L$, $T$, and
speed) use successful trials only.  For a condition with no successful trial,
we additionally report the mean collision/timeout-truncated observed path
$L_{\rm obs}$ as a diagnostic; it is never presented as a completed mission.
Language trials also report
route change, compliance, decision distance, switches, and exposure
$E_{\mathrm{sem}}=L^{-1}\int_0^T r(\vect{p}(t))v(t)\,dt$.  Profiling separates
point processing, graph construction, A*, planning, and PEARL latency.

\subsection{Offline Local-Policy Evaluation}
\textit{Protocol.} The final corridor-conditioned checkpoint is trained on
9,000 routes (8,100 training split) spanning cylindrical-tree, real-tree-cloud,
and block scenes, and is evaluated on 1,479 paired single-step routes of a
regression benchmark under a 10-m local-subgoal contract.  Inputs are paired
and initial motion is zero; primitives are sampled at 101 points with a
collision threshold of a 0.3-m robot radius plus a 0.2-m safety margin.  The
corridor-trained lineage was initialized from a checkpoint fine-tuned on this
benchmark's training split, so we report it as a regression result rather
than a fully independent test; the YOPO baseline never saw the benchmark.
This is not a closed-loop success test.

\textit{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:route_policy_results} shows that final
corridor conditioning reduces collision from $8.05\%$ to $0.14\%$ and
violation from $50.10\%$ to $25.22\%$, while progress rises from $6.225$ to
$6.502$~m and mean minimum clearance from $1.156$ to $1.335$~m: on this
benchmark the safety gain no longer trades off against progress.

\subsection{Persistence and Semantic-Exposure Analysis}
\textit{Protocol.} The graph and semantic memory stay alive during a
$(0,0,1.6)\rightarrow(0,140,1.6)\rightarrow(0,0,1.6)$~m flight.  Goal events
split the legs; 100-Hz odometry gives time and length, while insertion,
rebuild, and route-switch logs measure reuse.  World-frame clouds are cropped
to $z\in[0.3,3.2]$~m and voxelized at 0.5~m.  We measure occupied-cell density
within 3~m, distance to high-risk semantic nodes near flight height, and the
trajectory fraction within 5~m of them.

The pending causal replay holds graph snapshots fixed over
$\lambda_{\rm sem}\in\{0,1,2,4\}$ and $R_s\in\{4,8,12\}$~m, with
$\sigma_s=R_s/2$.  In particular, paired weights 2 and 0 separate semantic
preference from geometric sparsity.

\textit{Observed behavior.} Both available round trips reused the outbound
graph.  Insertions per rebuild fell by $62.7\%$ and $61.5\%$, and switches fell
18--12 and 15--10.  Rebuild mean nevertheless rose $21.5\%$ and $17.6\%$ as the
working set grew; path length rose $1.3\%$ and $4.6\%$.  On the second return,
mean/P90 density fell $8.2\%/28.6\%$, minimum semantic-node distance rose
2.56--6.93~m, and exposure within 5~m fell $8.7\%$--0.  This shows reuse and a
less cluttered, lower-exposure return, but opposite viewpoints and correlated
geometry prevent a semantic-causality claim without the paired replay.

\section{DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS}
ScaleNav separates reusable mapping, semantic route choice, and local execution.
The mapping layer is plug-and-play because its input and output contracts do
not depend on the local policy.  The demonstrated execution layer is not: its
online variant couples a frontier-conditioned YOPO policy to an
ordered-bubble MPC refinement, and its corridor-trained variant is augmented
and trained to consume the ordered corridor.
Far-field nodes are optimistic frontier hypotheses rather than safety
certificates.  Their candidate witnesses are checked against known geometry,
while unobserved-space safety still depends on subsequent depth and local
avoidance.

The semantic parameters have distinct and monotone roles.  Increasing
$\lambda_{\rm sem}$ strengthens preference for lower-risk routes without
changing their geometric validity, whereas increasing $R_s$ expands the
region influenced by each observation and simultaneously broadens its tied
Gaussian bandwidth.  Excessive values can therefore produce conservative
detours, while small values postpone the branch decision.  The fixed
sensitivity protocol above is needed to establish how wide the stable
operating range is; until those replay results are populated, robustness to
these parameters should not be inferred from the nominal setting alone.

SUPER and ScaleNav address different layers: SUPER provides geometric,
safety-assured high-speed execution, whereas ScaleNav provides persistent,
language-conditioned route choice.  Their composition is not evaluated here.

The current topological search uses a fixed-height layer, and the offline
benchmark uses zero initial motion rather than the online C++ M4 search.  A
fully 3-D closed-loop evaluation is future work.  The corridor-trained network
is evaluated offline only; all closed-loop flights use the MPC refinement
variant, so closing the loop with the trained corridor policy also remains
future work.  The semantic interface is
model-independent: boxes, points, or masks can be rasterized into the same
heatmap contract, but no alternative model is evaluated here.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{CONCLUSION}
ScaleNav organizes aerial navigation as three explicit layers.  A plug-and-play
persistent mapper turns depth and odometry into reusable verified free-space
topology.  A slow route layer combines this backbone with provisional
far-field semantic frontiers, continuous witness risk, and world-frame route
memory.  A fast corridor-conditioned policy receives the selected witness as
a frontier goal and ordered bubbles and retains control of the local primitive.
Thus mapping provides persistent structure, the route layer decides where to
go, and the execution layer decides how to fly.
