\begin{abstract}

Learning-based local planners fly fast and react quickly, but they are memoryless.
Their decision horizon is set by the depth range, not by the environment.
A building that must be circumnavigated is structurally out of reach for a planner that sees ten meters ahead.
We present ScaleNav, a two-layer system that gives a memoryless neural planner a spatial memory.
A slow layer maintains a persistent free-space graph in the world frame.
Collision-free spheres cover the observed free space; edges store witness paths.
The graph is validated every frame against the current depth image.
A goal-weighted rolling A* selects a route on this graph.
The route is compressed into a sphere-chain corridor and rendered as a depth-aligned mask channel.
A corridor-conditioned YOPO network consumes this channel together with the current depth and a task-progress vector.
Scale adaptation is carried entirely by this input.
The network structure, the weights, and all hyperparameters are shared across forests and city blocks.
Open-vocabulary semantics from a frozen vision-language model modulate only the graph edge costs.
The fast policy is never trained on any category.
Safety does not depend on semantics.
Experiments in simulation evaluate cross-scale flight, memory bandwidth, depth degradation, and open-vocabulary rerouting.

\end{abstract}

\section{INTRODUCTION}

Consider a quadrotor flying from point A to point B without a prior map.
It carries an RGB-D camera and onboard compute.
It must fly fast and continuously.
The environment spans two scales.
Forests contain gaps of one to three meters.
Walls and buildings impose detour topologies of tens of meters.

Learning-based local planners such as YOPO \cite{yopo} map a single depth frame directly to motion primitives.
They are fast and robust to dynamics.
But they have three structural limits.
First, the decision horizon equals the sensor horizon.
A turn around a building comes too late.
This is not a tuning issue; it is structural.
Second, the policy is memoryless.
The width of a building seen one frame ago is lost immediately.
At a fork with limited visibility, the policy can only guess.
Third, the policy has no notion of task progress.
The goal is clipped to the field of view.
The network cannot distinguish a mid-route point from the final goal, and it does not slow down near the goal.

Classical hierarchical methods build a map and run a global planner.
They handle large obstacles, but at a cost.
Mapping and replanning interrupts continuous flight.
Hybrid representations that treat large and small obstacles differently need a fragile scale threshold.
We avoid both.

Our design follows three principles.
First, scale adaptivity is carried entirely by the input; the model structure and parameters never change.
Second, all scenes share one set of hyperparameters.
Third, an executable action exists at all times; the degraded behavior is slowing down and looking while moving, not hovering.

ScaleNav realizes these principles with a fast--slow architecture (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}).
The slow layer keeps a persistent free-space graph in the world frame, built on the sphere-cover representation of EPIC \cite{epic}.
It adds visible-collision gating, speculative nodes, and goal-weighted search.
The fast layer is a corridor-conditioned YOPO network.
The interface between the two is a sphere-chain corridor rendered as a depth-aligned mask channel.
Open-vocabulary queries from a frozen PEARL model \cite{pearl} enter only as edge costs on the graph.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Memory as a spatial region.} We inject memory into a memoryless neural planner as a depth-aligned corridor channel. The network stays stateless. Training remains single-frame. No RNN and no frame stacking are needed.
\item \textbf{Depth-driven visible-collision gating.} Edges are blocked the moment the current depth explicitly observes a collision. Unobserved space stays optimistic. No extra unknown state is maintained.
\item \textbf{Decoupled open-vocabulary semantics.} Semantics modulate only graph edge costs. The policy is trained on pure geometry. A new query word at test time reroutes globally with zero retraining.
\item \textbf{Task-progress conditioning.} Two scalars, straight-line distance to goal and remaining corridor length, let one policy fly fast mid-route and slow down near the goal.
\end{itemize}

\section{RELATED WORK}

\subsection{Learning-Based Local Planners}

YOPO \cite{yopo} maps depth, body state, and a goal vector to primitive offsets and scores, trained with guidance learning.
Loquercio et al. \cite{loquercio} fly high-speed in the wild with a privileged expert and DAgger.
Depthnav \cite{depthnav} uses depth-only reinforcement learning.
These policies are reactive and memoryless.
Their goal input is a single vector, clipped to the sensor range.
ScaleNav keeps this fast reactive core and adds memory through the input, not through the network state.

\subsection{Memory, Mapping, and Global Planning}

NanoMap \cite{nanomap} keeps a short window of raw point clouds for proximity queries without fusing a global map.
We adopt the same stance: a short raw-point cache serves radius estimation and per-frame collision checks; no global fused map, no voxel occupancy, no ESDF is maintained.
FAR Planner \cite{far} and SPTM \cite{sptm} keep persistent route memory; we borrow the persistence principle.
Bubble Planner \cite{bubble} plans in receding corridors; we adopt its receding-horizon corridor cut.
RAPPIDS \cite{rappids} queries collisions directly in the depth image; our gating uses the same projection test.
EPIC \cite{epic} covers free space with spheres and maintains the graph incrementally for exploration.
We reuse its representation and maintenance, and replace frontier logic with goal-weighted search and speculative nodes.
SCOPE \cite{scope}, a concurrent work, uses a voxel belief map with lidar and trajectory optimization.
ScaleNav instead uses a persistent free-space graph, depth-gated validation, and a learned fast layer, and keeps no strict unknown state.

\subsection{Open-Vocabulary Semantics in Navigation}

CLIP \cite{clip} established image-level open-vocabulary recognition.
PEARL \cite{pearl} produces geometry-aligned open-vocabulary heatmaps from text queries.
VLFM \cite{vlfm} builds value maps from VLM similarity for frontier selection in indoor ObjectNav.
These systems train or tune the semantic behavior into a policy or a value map.
ScaleNav places semantics on the planner, not in the network.
Open vocabulary becomes a property by construction.

\section{SYSTEM OVERVIEW}

\subsection{Problem Statement}

The quadrotor flies from $\mathbf{p}_{\text{start}}$ to $\mathbf{p}_{\text{goal}}$ in a static, unknown environment.
Observations at time $t$ are a depth image $D_t \in \mathbb{R}^{96\times160}$, an RGB image $I_t$, and the body state $\mathbf{x}_t$.
The policy must produce dynamically feasible trajectories online.
All scenes use one set of hyperparameters.

\subsection{Architecture}

Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the data flow.
The slow layer runs at graph-update rate, outside the control loop.
It maintains a persistent free-space graph (Sec.~\ref{sec:graph}).
A rolling goal-weighted A* selects a route.
The near segment of the route is exposed as a sphere-chain corridor (Sec.~\ref{sec:corridor}).
The fast layer runs in the control loop.
It fuses three inputs: the current depth, the rendered corridor mask, and an observation vector with task progress.
It outputs offsets and scores for 15 motion primitives (Sec.~\ref{sec:policy}).
A frozen open-vocabulary model modulates graph edge costs (Sec.~\ref{sec:semantics}).

\subsection{Where Memory Lives}

The network is stateless.
Memory lives in the world-frame graph.
Each frame, memory is compressed into a channel aligned with the depth image.
Training stays single-frame; the simulator pipeline is unchanged.
This turns the ablation axis into a \emph{memory bandwidth} question: goal vector, waypoint, or corridor region, fed to the same network through the same pipeline.

\section{PERSISTENT FREE-SPACE GRAPH}
\label{sec:graph}

\subsection{Graph as Memory}

We build no global point cloud, no voxel occupancy, and no ESDF.
A short raw-point cache in the style of NanoMap \cite{nanomap} serves radius estimation and current-frame collision queries.
Unseen space stays optimistic and traversable.
Only an explicit collision in the current depth blocks a node or an edge.

A node is $v_i = (\mathbf{c}_i, r_i, q_i)$: sphere center, radius, and confidence.
The radius is the distance to the nearest obstacle, estimated from a KD-tree over the back-projected points of the current depth frame.
It is computed once per new node.

An edge is $e_{ij} = (\pi_{ij}, L_{ij}, d_{\text{safe}}, q_{ij}, s_{ij})$: a witness path found by A* on the local point set at generation time, its length, the minimum clearance along it, a confidence, and a state.
When an edge is reused, $\pi_{ij}$ is re-validated against the current frame.
A failed validation downgrades or disconnects the edge.

Graph maintenance follows the incremental scheme of EPIC \cite{epic}.
A spatial hash manages candidate regions.
A union--find clusters spheres into representative vertices.
Each frame computes the difference $V_{\text{pre}}/V_{\text{new}}$ and touches only the changed local part.
The graph persists in the world frame and accumulates across frames.

No artificial goal node is created.
The goal acts only as a search direction.
This avoids repeatedly deleting and rebuilding an artificial terminal, which would flip the homotopy of left- versus right-hand detours.

\subsection{Visible-Collision Gating}

Each new depth frame triggers a projection check on the active route and on candidate edges.
Sampled points along $\pi_{ij}$ are projected into the depth image and compared with the pixel depth.
An explicit collision blocks the edge.
Unobserved space creates no unknown state; the edge remains an optimistic candidate.
This gating is the only mechanism that couples the graph to the sensor, and it is conservative in exactly one direction: seen obstacles block, unseen space does not.

\subsection{Goal-Weighted Rolling Search}

Search runs on the real graph reachable from the current odometry node.
For each reachable node $v$ we compute
\begin{equation}
J(v) = \lVert \mathbf{c}_v - \mathbf{p}_{\text{goal}} \rVert + \alpha\, g(v),
\end{equation}
where $g(v)$ is the accumulated edge cost on the graph.
The reachable node with the smallest $J$ becomes the rolling terminal, and the topological path is recovered from the search parents.
The edge cost is
\begin{equation}
C(e) = w_{\text{len}} L_{ij} + w_{\text{risk}} \, d_{\text{safe}}^{-1} + \lambda\, C_{\text{sem}}(e),
\end{equation}
with $C_{\text{sem}}$ from Sec.~\ref{sec:semantics}.
Blocked edges are removed; unobserved regions are not penalized.
As the graph expands, the rolling terminal advances toward the goal naturally.

The near segment of the path yields the waypoint.
When the real-graph terminal enters the local connection range of the final goal, a bubble A* validates the last segment, and the exact goal is appended to the witness path as a terminal coordinate.
It is never a persistent graph node.

\subsection{Receding-Horizon Route Identity}

Node objects are replaced by the incremental update, so pointers cannot carry route identity across cycles.
We store the previous witness path in world coordinates, project the vehicle onto it, and keep a fixed-length receding window ahead of the projection.
Real edges near this window are re-identified as the previous route and reused at zero cost.
The previous rolling terminal is re-mapped to the current graph by world coordinates.
Search toward the goal resumes only when the terminal is reached, deleted, or unreachable.
What persists is the free-space corridor and the detour homotopy, not volatile object addresses.
No subgoal low-pass filter, no left--right switching threshold, and no artificial-node rule is added.

\subsection{Applicability of the Sphere Primitive}

The sphere primitive assumes no gaps narrower than the vehicle.
Buildings and walls satisfy this.
Tree trunks leave gaps of 1--3 m, which a vehicle-scale sphere chain covers.
The price is a conservative radius estimate near thin trunks, hence a narrower corridor.
We accept this; Experiment C exposes the effect.
For truly narrow gaps, the region generator can be swapped for the direction-aware convex regions of FRGraph \cite{frgraph} without changing maintenance, gating, search, or the corridor interface.

The forward-facing camera covers only the front in one frame.
The back and the sides rely on graph persistence.
Coverage gaps after takeoff and after sharp turns are treated optimistically and corrected by gating on approach.

\section{CORRIDOR INTERFACE}
\label{sec:corridor}

\subsection{Sphere-Chain Corridor}

The corridor is a sphere chain along the global route, parameterized by arc length:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}(s) = \{ \mathbf{p}(s), r(s), d(s) \},
\end{equation}
with centerline position, local radius, and remaining distance to the terminal.
We do not build a separate polyhedron corridor; the sphere chain is itself the corridor interface to the policy.

For a sample $\mathbf{c}_k$ on the selected witness path, the effective radius is
\begin{equation}
r_k = \max\bigl(0,\; d_{\text{occ}}(\mathbf{c}_k) - d_{\text{margin}}\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $d_{\text{occ}}$ is the distance to known obstacles and $d_{\text{margin}}$ the safety margin.
If $\lVert \mathbf{c}_k - \mathbf{c}_{k+1} \rVert \le r_k + r_{k+1}$ fails, intermediate spheres are inserted.

Spheres are sampled every half local radius along the witness path, not only at nodes.
Node spheres alone leave gaps between clusters, and a single interval mask would fill those gaps as solid.
Filling along the witness path removes this artifact; corridor fidelity follows the witness path, not the node density.

The corridor exists in the graph before the search; the search only selects and orders it.
It is cached in the world frame.
When the graph does not change, the corridor does not move; each frame applies only a coordinate transform and re-renders by analytic ray--sphere intersection.

The corridor span is the near segment of the A* route plus an optimistic segment within the receding window.
For the policy the corridor is a soft guidance.
An optimistic segment that meets an explicit obstacle in the current depth is removed at re-search; no unknown state is maintained.

\subsection{Waypoints and Execution}

The A* output is a node sequence; each edge carries its witness points.
The execution path concatenates the witness paths, applies one cross-edge shortcut under the sphere-overlap condition, and publishes a rolling subgoal along the decimated path.
The shortcut removes a middle point only when the first and last safety spheres overlap, so a curved path around a wall is never straightened into a centerline.
The subgoal is a rolling control target, not a completion signal.
The final hover state is entered only when the subgoal coincides with the goal, the position error is below $0.5$ m, and the speed is below $0.3$ m/s.

\section{CORRIDOR-CONDITIONED POLICY}
\label{sec:policy}

\subsection{Inputs and Outputs}

The fast policy consumes three inputs.
The \emph{depth channel} ($1\times96\times160$) is the current observation; it answers what is around now.
The \emph{corridor mask channel} ($2\times96\times160$) stores, per pixel, the depth interval $[near, far]$ in which the corresponding ray crosses the corridor.
The interval is normalized by the maximum range, truncated at the depth surface, not truncated at NaN pixels, and zero where no corridor exists.
It answers where the remembered route goes and how wide it is.
The \emph{observation vector} holds velocity (3), acceleration (3), the local waypoint (3), the straight-line distance to the final goal (1), and the remaining corridor length (1).
It answers how far the task has progressed.

The output is unchanged from YOPO: offsets and scores of 15 primitives on a $5\times3$ lattice.
The backbone is a ResNet-18 with the first convolution widened to the extra channels.
New channels are initialized by copying the pretrained depth-channel weights, so the corridor contributes zero at initialization and training starts from the original behavior.

\subsection{Why Two Channels}

The two image channels share the carrier but not the semantics.
Depth is perception of the current frame.
The corridor is a decision computed from accumulated observations.
After truncation, the corridor still carries three things depth does not: memory beyond the field of view and range (turn early), a topological choice at symmetric forks, and a fallback under degradation (the corridor renders where depth is NaN).
The redundant cases, open space and straight flight, are exactly the fallback to the original behavior.
The value appears where the two channels disagree.
They must stay separate.
Training lets the collision cost dominate the corridor cost, so the priority ``depth for safety, corridor for direction'' emerges without hand-written rules.

\subsection{Training}

We follow the differentiable-cost route of YOPOv2-Tracker \cite{yopotracker}; no expert demonstration is used.
The total cost is fully differentiable:
an ESDF collision cost for hard safety,
a smoothness cost,
a goal cost,
a corridor cost, and
an arrival cost.
The corridor violation of a trajectory point $\mathbf{p}$ against the chain is
\begin{equation}
\ell_{\text{corr}}(\mathbf{p}) = \min_k \max\bigl(0,\; \lVert \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{c}_k \rVert - r_k\bigr),
\end{equation}
differentiable and back-propagated through the chain.
Its weight is kept low: the corridor guides the direction and never overrides the collision cost.
The arrival term penalizes terminal speed and overshoot when the remaining distance drops below a threshold.

In simulation the corridor is computed offline from the ground-truth map as a privileged corridor.
At test time it is replaced by the real graph output.
Corridor perturbation augmentation closes the domain gap: radius noise, random segment dropout, and global translation jitter.
This is isomorphic to depth-corruption augmentation: dirt seen in training is not fatal at test time.

Five data disciplines shape the distribution:
(1) samples where the corridor deliberately disagrees with the goal direction, or the network learns to ignore the channel;
(2) all-zero corridor samples, so the policy falls back to the original YOPO behavior when the graph has no route;
(3) remaining-distance coverage from hundreds of meters to half a meter, or the progress scalars carry no causal signal;
(4) dynamic obstacles inside the corridor, so the hard priority of depth is actually trained;
(5) corridor rendered where depth is missing, training the depth--corridor division of labor.

The training data is fully decoupled from semantics: geometry and corridor in, trajectory out, no category anywhere.

\section{OPEN-VOCABULARY GLOBAL GUIDANCE}
\label{sec:semantics}

The user issues a text query, e.g., ``avoid the buildings.''
The frozen PEARL model \cite{pearl} produces a heatmap of that category on the RGB frame.
Heatmap pixels are projected to the world frame through depth.
Edges sample the heatmap along their witness paths and accumulate a semantic edge cost $C_{\text{sem}}(e)$.
The total edge cost of (2) changes; the A* route changes.
The fast policy only receives a different corridor.
It never learns that the word ``building'' exists.

Open vocabulary is therefore a constructed property.
The only trained component is the fast policy, and its data is pure geometry.
PEARL is open-vocabulary by design.
The graph is a search structure and is not trained.
No component is bound to a category at training time.
A query word never seen before works at test time, without relying on network generalization.

The boundary is stated explicitly: semantics participate only in route cost, never in geometric collision checking.
Safety does not depend on semantics.
This boundary is a feature, not a limitation.

\section{EXPERIMENTS (PLANNED)}
\label{sec:experiments}

All experiments run in the Colosseum simulator \cite{airsim} with onboard RGB-D.
All scenes share one set of hyperparameters, stated in the source release.
Baselines are the original YOPO \cite{yopo}, depthnav \cite{depthnav}, a classical FAR-Planner \cite{far} plus local planner stack, and SCOPE \cite{scope} if code is available.
Metrics are success rate, collision rate, path length, completion time, minimum clearance, and replanning frequency.

\subsection{Experiment A: Cross-Scale Flight}

Scenes: empty, sparse trees, forest, long wall, single building, building blocks, and a mixed-scale single continuous flight (forest interleaved with buildings, one run, constant speed, no mode flag).
We compare the original YOPO against graph-plus-YOPO.
We expect YOPO to succeed in forest-scale scenes and to fail at building-scale detours, and ScaleNav to close that gap without changing parameters.

\subsection{Experiment B: Memory Bandwidth Ablation}

Three arms share one network structure and one training pipeline: direct goal, waypoint, and corridor mask.
The only variable is the amount of memory injected into the fast layer.
A dedicated ``corridor disagrees with goal'' scene tests whether the network reads the corridor channel instead of ignoring it.
We expect the corridor arm to dominate the waypoint arm on building-scale scenes, which would show that the gain is not merely ``one more point.''

\subsection{Experiment C: Degradation Sweep}

Depth dropout, noise, and fog are increased in levels.
We plot success rate and speed against the degradation level.
The ablated system without graph and corridor is expected to collide at high degradation, while the corridor provides direction where depth fails.
This experiment also exposes the conservative-radius effect near thin trunks.

\subsection{Experiment D: Open-Vocabulary Rerouting}

On the graph, we switch the query word: no semantics, ``avoid buildings,'' ``avoid trees,'' ``avoid power lines.''
We compare the induced global routes.
The fast layer is not retrained at all.
We expect geometrically feasible but semantically distinct routes, demonstrating that task intent that geometry cannot express is carried by the edge costs.

\section{CONCLUSIONS}

ScaleNav gives a memoryless neural planner a spatial memory without touching its structure.
A persistent free-space graph remembers; a sphere-chain corridor rendered as a depth-aligned channel injects that memory into the policy.
Scale adaptivity is carried by the input, not by parameters, thresholds, or mode switches.
Open-vocabulary semantics modulate only graph edge costs, keeping safety independent of semantics and retraining at zero.
Planned experiments will quantify cross-scale flight, memory bandwidth, degradation robustness, and open-vocabulary rerouting.
Future work closes the loop from route back to graph growth, using the current route tangent as a candidate direction for graph refinement.
